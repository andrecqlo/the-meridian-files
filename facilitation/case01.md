# Case 01 — The Evidence · facilitator notes

**Do not circulate this file to players.** It contains the solutions and the
reason the game exists.

## What this actually is

Case 01 is an equality, diversity and inclusion session disguised as an
investigation game. The words never appear until the debrief. Players spend
fifteen minutes doing rigorous analytical work — checking who a sample
represented, whose challenge was heard, what an average hid, which claims were
assumptions, whether a small group still mattered — and only then are told that
what they have been doing has a name.

The reveal is the point. It works because the players did the work first, on
their own terms, and are then credited for it. It stops working the moment
anyone walks in knowing.

### Framing, before the session

Say: *"It's an investigation game. Fifteen minutes, one screen, you work as a
team."*

Do not say: it's for fun · it's a team-building exercise · it's training · it's
about inclusion, unconscious bias, or anything of that family. Do not use the
word "training" in the calendar invite. "Team activity — The Meridian Files
(15 mins)" is enough.

**The reveal works once per office.** People talk. Schedule teams close
together — ideally the same day, at worst the same week — and ask people who
have played not to describe the ending. Once it has gone round the building,
the case still teaches, but as an ordinary exercise rather than a reveal.

## Learning objectives

For whoever has to approve this as training, in plain language:

1. Recognise that a sampling or distribution decision can exclude a group
   before any analysis begins, and that weighting cannot recover people who
   were never asked.
2. Identify how an aggregate figure can be simultaneously accurate and
   misleading when the population behind it differs from the population a
   decision will affect.
3. Distinguish evidenced claims from assumptions in an impact assessment, and
   trace a claim to its source.
4. Judge materiality separately from prevalence — a small percentage can be a
   large number of people with a high cost of failure.
5. Notice how a challenge raised in a meeting can be recorded as agreement, and
   what that costs the decision.
6. Understand that exclusion frequently runs along mundane operational
   variables — a contact-preference field, a channel, a form — rather than
   along a protected characteristic, and that those variables correlate with
   protected characteristics without being them.

## Session shape — 15 to 17 minutes

Challenge 1 was rebuilt to a two-document, one-lock loop and now runs shorter
than it used to. Typical elapsed time against the 15:00 clock:

| Time | What is happening |
|---|---|
| 0:00–1:00 | Intro screen. One person reads the six lines aloud. |
| 1:00–3:00 | Challenge 1 — the customer file, the reply, the reference sheet in the drawer, the laptop's PIN lock. |
| 3:00–6:00 | Challenge 2 — the reweighting workbench. |
| 6:00–9:00 | Challenge 3 — the pinboard. |
| 9:00–11:00 | Passphrase, Sam's memo, the decision, the £900k twist. |
| 11:00–13:00 | Debrief: tally, chain, reveal, Blind Spot Check. |
| +5 minutes | Sector prompts as a conversation. Not on the clock. |

That is roughly 13:45 against a 15:00 clock, and fast teams land nearer
10–11 minutes. The slack sits in the untimed debrief, not the countdown — if a
team finishes early, let them sit with the reveal rather than rushing it.

One person drives. Everyone else reads and argues. If the group is larger than
about six, split into two screens and compare memos at the end.

## The five findings, and what each one teaches

| # | Finding | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The sampling frame — 9,214 never invited | Who is missing from the evidence, and why weighting cannot fix it |
| 2 | The aggregation — 81% becomes 73% on the service's mix | What an average hides when the population changes |
| 3 | The unsupported assumptions — two claims resting on nothing | Assumptions about groups, presented as evidence |
| 4 | The 3% — 7,200 people at 6× handling time | Materiality is not prevalence |
| 5 | What happened to Priya — hidden, optional | Whose challenge gets heard, and how minutes launder it |

Findings 1–4 are awarded on completing the challenges. Finding 5 is only
awarded if the team finds the workshop minutes under the torch and rebuilds
Priya's sentence. Most teams miss it. That is deliberate: the debrief shows it
to them as the one they missed, which lands harder than finding it would have.

## How the room works

Case 01 is a point-and-click room. The team clicks objects on Sam's desk, and
objects open in sequence rather than all at once:

| Object | Opens when |
|---|---|
| Paper tray | from the start |
| UV torch, lying on the desk | from the start |
| Top drawer | from the start — a single printed reference sheet |
| Laptop | always clickable, but shows a PIN lock until Challenge 1 is solved |
| Pilot data — a folder on the laptop | once the laptop is unlocked |
| Bottom drawer | Challenge 2 is solved — it yields a ball of string |
| Pinboard | the team is carrying the string |
| READ_BEFORE_SIGNOFF | all three words are stamped |

Clicking something that will not open yet is not a dead end: it puts a line in
the status bar under the room, in Sam's voice, saying what is missing. Clicking
the pinboard without the string says so in as many words. **Nothing is ever
hidden behind a search** — if a team seems stuck, tell them to click things.

The torch is the one object worth pointing at if a team has not picked it up by
about four minutes in. Sam's annotations are all read by moving its light across
a page, and a team that never takes it will miss every one of them.

### Two marking systems, one fiction

The room uses two visible-annotation systems that look similar and are meant
to: a highlighter, and UV ink. This is deliberate, not an inconsistency to
flag if a player asks. Sam highlighted two things openly — "Post only" on the
customer's profile, and "issued by email to all our customers" on the reply —
because a highlighter is what Sam could still do without anyone noticing.
Everything Sam actually thought but could not say out loud went into the UV
ink instead, which is why the intro line is "I couldn't say them out loud."
The letter and the reply both carry a visible tease ("something is smeared
across the paper") until the torch actually finds the note underneath — taking
the torch is not the same as knowing where to point it, and the tease exists
so a team that is carrying the torch but hasn't found the note yet still has a
reason to keep sweeping.

## Solutions

- **Challenge 1.** The customer, Mrs H. Wiśniewska, prefers post only —
  highlighted on her profile in the paper tray. The reply says the survey was
  issued by email to all customers — highlighted in the reply. The reference
  sheet in the (unlocked) top drawer gives the split: 9,214 of 51,214
  customers (18%) were post only, meaning the email invitation could never
  have reached them. **9,214** is the laptop's PIN. A wrong first attempt
  sharpens Sam's sticky-note clue from "the ones nobody asked" to "how many
  customers were never sent the survey?", so a team that skips the drawer
  still has a way in. Unlocking stamps WHO.
- **Challenge 2.** Region is a dead end and says so. Age is the trap: it shows
  a real population difference and reweighting it lands back on 81, because the
  survey was already weighted for age — that is a finding, not a bug. Case
  complexity is the one: drag the mix from 62/28/10 to the service's 35/40/25
  and the figure lands on **73**, below the current service's 76. Stamps IS.
- **Challenge 3.** String each claim to its evidence — support tickets, coverage
  map, the travel assumption, the vendor brochure, the assisted-service
  analysis — then flag *disabled users (removes travel)* and *non-English
  speakers (automated translation)* as not supported. Stamps MISSING.
- **Passphrase.** The three stamped words: **WHO IS MISSING**. Spacing, hyphens
  and capitals are all forgiven.
- **The string.** In the bottom drawer, locked until Challenge 2 lands. Take
  it, then click the pinboard.
- **Priya, optional.** Put the torch over the workshop minutes on the pinboard
  after the board is complete, then reassemble: *"My concern is the people who
  have never used the digital service — not the ones using it more."*

## Hints and pacing — you should not need to help

There is no hint menu and no penalty. Sam's notes surface on their own: tier 1
after 90 seconds without meaningful progress, one tier per further 60 seconds,
so a stuck team holds the near-explicit tier by 3:30.

Behind that sits an invisible pace line — letter opened by 13:30, Challenge 1
by 11:00, Challenge 2 by 7:30, pinboard by 5:00, passphrase by 4:00, decision
by 1:30. A team more than a minute behind gets hints twice as fast; more than
two minutes behind, the next note surfaces in the margin, pulsing, unopened;
more than three minutes behind, that note opens itself once and can be put
back. Pacing never completes a step: the selection, the slider and the sentence
are always the team's own work.

So: let them struggle. If you must intervene, read Sam's note aloud rather than
explaining it yourself.

## Running the debrief

Read the tally down the screen. Let the missed line land — do not soften it.

The reveal paragraph should be read aloud, by someone, slowly. Do not
paraphrase it, do not add to it, and in particular do not add a joke to relieve
the moment. The line that does the work is *"Most training asks people to spot
the obvious. You just did the hard version."*

Expect one of two reactions. Most groups go quiet and then start telling you
about their own evidence. A few say "so it was a trick". If that happens, the
answer is: nothing was hidden from you, you found all of it, and the only thing
withheld was the label.

The coda after the reveal matters as much as the reveal. The gap in this case
does not run along a protected characteristic — it runs along a
contact-preference field. That is the realistic case and the harder one to
spot, and it is why "we checked the protected characteristics" is not the same
as having checked.

Then the Blind Spot Check, then the sector prompts. Run the prompts as a
five-minute conversation off the clock — that is where the session actually
converts into behaviour. Finish by having someone download the memo and put it
somewhere the team will see it again.

## If you are asked

Yes, this is EDI training. It maps to sampling and representation, evidence
quality, psychological safety in meetings, and materiality of impact on small
groups. The concealment is a pedagogical device, not a trick: nothing is
hidden from the players except the label, and the label is given to them in
full, in one place, with credit.
